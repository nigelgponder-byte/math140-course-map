# Research

Back to [00 Start Here](../00%20Start%20Here.md).

These sources support the direction of our design. They do not prove that Stepping Stone works. That is what the classroom trial is for.

**Checked Nov 12, 2026.** Every source below was checked against its official record (Crossref, PubMed, ERIC, or the publisher's page), and every finding was checked against the abstract or article. Before you quote a number on a slide, it must appear in the "What it found" column.

## The 3 core studies
| Study | What it found | What it means for us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bastani et al. 2025** (*PNAS*) | Nearly 1,000 high school math students. With GPT-4 during practice, grades went up (**48%** plain ChatGPT, **127%** safeguarded tutor). Once access was taken away, the plain-ChatGPT group did **17% worse** than students who never had it. The safeguards **largely mitigated** that harm. Students used plain GPT-4 as a "crutch". | Our helper gives hints only. No AI at the quiz (Step 5), because that is where the harm showed up. Weak proofs always get a hint, never a pass. |
| **Kestin et al. 2025** (*Scientific Reports*) | A randomized trial with **194** students in Harvard's intro physics class. Median learning gains with a purpose-built AI tutor were **more than double** those of an active-learning class, in less time (median **49 min**). Students felt more engaged and more motivated. The paper warns AI tutors show "uncanny confidence" when wrong, so the team fed the tutor written step-by-step solutions. | One hint and one question back, like a good tutor. Our answer key (accepted reasons) keeps hints accurate, the same idea as Kestin's written solutions. |
| **Petrov et al. 2025** (arXiv preprint) | Experts graded AI proofs on the 2025 USA Math Olympiad. **Gemini 2.5 Pro scored 25%; every other model scored under 5%.** The models sounded sure anyway. | The AI never grades and never says "correct". The Professor decides. |

## More sources we can use
| Source | What it found | What it means for us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Steenbergen-Hu & Cooper 2013** (*Journal of Educational Psychology*) | 26 reports, 34 samples (1997–2010). Computer math tutors had "no negative and perhaps a small positive effect" on K–12 math (g = 0.01 to 0.09). Effects were bigger when the program ran **less than a school year**. They helped **low achievers less** than other students. | Run a short trial, not a year-long rollout. Own the low-achiever risk: the paper route counts the same, stuck students get flagged to the Professor, and the trial checks who is left out. |
| **Chi et al. 1989** (*Cognitive Science*) | Physics students studying worked examples. "Good" students explained each step to themselves and tied it to a principle, and learned with understanding. "Poor" students explained little and leaned on the examples. | Hints ask for a **reason**, not an answer. Step 4 (Explain) has students say the reason out loud. |
| **Alfredo et al. 2024** (*Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence*) | A review of 108 papers on AI and learning-analytics tools in education. Leaving students and teachers out of design can lead to mistrust. It recommends balancing human control against automation, and treating safety, reliability and trustworthiness as design principles. Few projects involved end users. | The Professor controls (kill switch, hint level, flag threshold). The trial's student feedback card. |
| **UNESCO 2023** (Miao & Holmes, *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*) | Calls for a human-centred approach that ensures "ethical, safe, equitable and meaningful use". Recommends mandatory data-privacy protection and an age limit for chatting with AI alone. Warns schools are largely unprepared to validate AI tools. | Usernames only, no real names. A trial before any wider rollout. |
| **U.S. Dept. of Education 2024** (*National Educational Technology Plan*, ERIC ED641164) | Built around three digital divides: **access, design and use**. Warns that edtech tools sometimes promise results without independent, research-based evidence. | The paper route counts the same, so no student needs a device. We test before we promise. |

## Use with care
- **Chi 1989** compares strong and weak students. It does not show that prompting explanations *causes* learning. Say "students who explained steps to themselves learned with understanding."
- **Steenbergen-Hu** studied older computer tutors (before ChatGPT), not hints-only AI. Say "computer tutors for math".
- **Petrov** is a preprint, not peer-reviewed, and Olympiad proofs are far harder than Math 140 proofs. The point is that AI sounds sure even when it is wrong.
- **UNESCO** and **the education plan** do not mention paper cards or office hours. "A non-device path reduces exclusion" is our conclusion from what they say.
- **Kestin** studied physics, and **Bastani** studied high school students. Neither is geometry at SCAD.

## Checked, but not used now
| Source | Why not |
| --- | --- |
| **Garzón & Acevedo 2019** (*Educational Research Review*). AR meta-analysis, 64 studies. | Stepping Stone has no AR feature. "Medium effect" not confirmed from the abstract. |
| **Wittenberg et al. 2025** (*PNAS Nexus*). AI-image labels, 7,579 Americans. | About misleading images on social media. Too far from a hint disclaimer to cite. |

## Don't say
- "Under 5% for all models" (Petrov). The best model scored 25%.
- "Without impairing conceptual retention" or "accelerates problem-solving stamina" (Bastani). The paper says the harm was "largely mitigated", measured by exam grades.
- "Compared to passive instruction" (Kestin). It was compared to *active* learning.
- "Teacher-built" (Kestin). Say "purpose-built".
- Any quote from the professor interview, unless the professor confirms it.

## Still missing
- **Onboarding interests.** Lead: Walkington 2013, on matching math problems to student interests.
- **Reward videos.** Lead: Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999. It warns expected rewards can lower motivation, so we need a design reason.
- **Step 1 (Draw) and Step 2 (Write).** Lead: research on the generation effect.
- **Step 4 (Explain), causal.** Lead: Chi et al. 1994.
- **Professor time.** Lead: instructor workload and early-alert dashboards.
- **Privacy.** Lead: FERPA.

None of these leads have been checked yet.
